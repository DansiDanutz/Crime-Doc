# STATE 3 — Script

**Target length:** 5 minutes ≈ 750 words

---

It's just after midnight, the 26th of September 1983. In a bunker south of Moscow, a man named Stanislav Petrov pours tea from a steel thermos and lowers himself into a chair facing a wall of screens. He isn't supposed to be here tonight. Someone else called in sick.

This is the story of how one man, in twenty-three minutes, decided whether the world would end.

The bunker is called Serpukhov-15. It is the brain of a system called Oko — Russian for "eye." Oko is a network of satellites that stare down at the United States, watching for the heat bloom of a launched missile. If America fires, Oko sees it first. Petrov's job is simple. Watch the screens. Trust the system. Report what it tells him.

The Cold War is at its lowest point in twenty years. Three weeks earlier, a Soviet fighter shot down a Korean passenger jet, killing 269 people. The Americans are running military exercises near Soviet borders. Both sides have their fingers near the button, and everyone is waiting for the other side to move first.

At fourteen minutes past midnight, the system moves.

A single word fills the main panel in red letters: LAUNCH. A siren begins to wail. The computer reports one intercontinental ballistic missile, fired from a base in the United States, climbing now, aimed at the Soviet Union.

Petrov has a protocol. He is to pick up the phone, call his superiors, and report a confirmed attack. From that call the chain runs upward in minutes — to the General Staff, to the men who can order a counterstrike of a thousand warheads. The doctrine is called launch on warning. You do not wait for the first bomb to land. You fire while the enemy's missiles are still in the air.

But Petrov hesitates.

Something about the picture is wrong. One missile. The Americans have thousands. A real first strike would be a wall of them, fired all at once, to destroy Soviet rockets before they ever left the ground. Nobody starts a nuclear war with a single shot. It makes no sense.

He has seconds. If the missile is real, it lands in about twenty minutes.

He picks up the phone and reports a system malfunction. A false alarm. He has no way to know if he is right.

Then the system reports a second missile. Then a third. A fourth. A fifth. The screen now reads five inbound warheads. The siren does not stop. Every protocol, every machine in the room, is telling him the attack is real and that he is burning the only minutes that matter.

Petrov holds. He reports it again as a malfunction. And then he sits, and he waits, and he watches the clock, knowing that if he is wrong, the first detonations will arrive in minutes, and he will have spent the last quiet moments of the world being stubborn.

The minutes pass.

Nothing happens. No flash. No call. The missiles that were never there never arrive. Ground radar — which can only see a missile once it crosses the horizon — shows an empty sky.

Petrov was right.

Later, the engineers find the cause. The Oko satellites had caught sunlight glinting off high clouds over the northern United States and read the reflection as the heat of a launch. A rare alignment of the sun, the satellites, and the autumn weather. The system did exactly what it was built to do. It just saw the wrong thing.

Petrov is not given a medal. A medal would mean admitting the system had failed. He is quietly reprimanded for a gap in his logbook during the incident — he was, he later said, holding a phone in one hand and an intercom in the other. He is reassigned. He takes early retirement. For ten years, no one outside that bunker knows his name.

He dies in 2017, in a small apartment outside Moscow, largely forgotten. The system he overruled was never the problem, his superiors maintained. The man was just doing his job.

But the system is still there. Some version of it sits in every nuclear state on Earth, scanning the sky, deciding in seconds what takes the rest of us a lifetime to understand. It is faster than us now. It is trusted more than us.

And the next time it sees something that isn't there, the man in the chair may decide to believe it.
