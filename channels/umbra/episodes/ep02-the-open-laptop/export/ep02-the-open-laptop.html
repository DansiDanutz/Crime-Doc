STATE 2 — Idea

Working title: The Open Laptop

Subject: On 1 October 2013, the FBI arrested Ross Ulbricht — "Dread Pirate Roberts," who

ran the dark-web marketplace Silk Road — in a San Francisco library, seizing his laptop while

it was still open and logged in.

Why it fits the DNA: Dark-web/hacking territory + a procedural capture. Exercises the full

mandatory ensemble: a main subject (red), secondary subjects (a moderator, library patrons),

police (FBI investigators), and special police who solve it (the undercover arrest team).

Cold-open moment: 1 October 2013, the science-fiction section of the Glen Park public

library, San Francisco — a young man sits, opens a laptop, and logs in.

The mechanism: Two years of anonymity (Tor + Bitcoin) → the load-bearing slip (an "altoid"

forum post in 2011 listing rossulbricht@gmail.com) → the problem of encryption (the laptop

locks the instant it closes) → the plan built around three seconds (a staged lovers' quarrel

Ending type: 1 + 2 — the marketplace is gone but the man is not (2025 pardon), and the next

Dread Pirate Roberts has read exactly how the first was caught. Last line lands on "word."

Key numbers/specifics: 1 Oct 2013; Glen Park library; ~$1.2 billion in sales; ran 2011–2013;

username "altoid"; rossulbricht@gmail.com; 29 years old; convicted 2015 on 7 counts; two life

terms + 40 years, no parole; six alleged murder-for-hire payments (no bodies, all scams);

full pardon January 2025.

STATE 3 — Script

Target length: 5 minutes ≈ 750 words

It's the 1st of October, 2013. In the science-fiction section of a public library in San Francisco, a young man sits down at a table by the window, opens a laptop, and begins to type. His name is Ross Ulbricht. He is 29 years old. He looks like a graduate student.

This is the story of how the FBI caught the most wanted man on the internet, and the three seconds that made it possible.

For two years, a website called Silk Road has sold almost anything illegal you can imagine. Drugs, mostly. It runs on the dark web, reachable only through Tor, a browser that bounces your connection around the world until no one can say where you are. Payment is in Bitcoin, hard to trace. By 2013 it has handled more than a billion dollars in sales.

The man who runs it is known only as Dread Pirate Roberts. He is a ghost. He gives orders, settles disputes, takes a cut of every sale. No one knows his real name. No one knows what country he is in. The FBI has been hunting him for two years and has almost nothing.

Then they find a thread.

Back in 2011, on a forum for drug enthusiasts, a user named "altoid" posted about a brand-new marketplace called Silk Road. It was one of the first mentions anywhere. A few months later, "altoid" posted again, this time looking to hire a programmer, asking interested people to get in touch. The message listed an email address. The address was rossulbricht@gmail.com.

It is the kind of mistake that takes a year to notice and a second to make. By the time anyone connects the dots, Ross Ulbricht has no idea his real name is sitting in a public forum, two clicks from the website he built.

The FBI now has a name, a face, and a city. But that is not enough. Dread Pirate Roberts runs Silk Road from an encrypted laptop. The moment the lid closes, the screen locks, and the encryption becomes effectively unbreakable. To prove he is Dread Pirate Roberts, they need to catch him with the laptop open, logged in, mid-session, and take it before he can touch a single key.

So they plan around three seconds.

On October 1st, agents follow Ulbricht into the Glen Park branch library. He sits down and logs in to Silk Road as the administrator. Behind him, two agents begin to argue. They are posing as a couple in the middle of a breakup, and the fight gets loud. Ulbricht does the natural thing. He turns to look.

In that moment, a third agent reaches past his shoulder, lifts the open laptop, and passes it to another agent who is already typing, keeping it awake, keeping it unlocked. Ulbricht turns back to an empty table.

On the screen is the back end of Silk Road. He is logged in as Dread Pirate Roberts. A chat window is open. He has just typed the words confirming he is the administrator. The ghost has a face now, and the face is sitting in a library with its hands still warm.

What they find on the laptop is everything. A journal. A logbook of the whole operation. Hundreds of thousands of dollars in Bitcoin. And files suggesting that, at least six times, Dread Pirate Roberts had paid to have people killed: rivals, a blackmailer, a former employee. No bodies were ever found. The hits, it turned out, were scams. He had paid for murders that never happened.

At trial, prosecutors do not even need the killings. The marketplace is enough. In 2015, Ross Ulbricht is convicted on seven counts and sentenced to two life terms in prison, plus forty years, with no possibility of parole. He is 31. The judge calls Silk Road a carefully planned life's work.

For the next decade he sits in a cell, the textbook example of what the dark web costs you when the laptop closes half a second too late.

And then, in January 2025, the President of the United States grants him a full pardon. After eleven years, Ross Ulbricht walks out of prison a free man.

The marketplace is gone. The man who built it is not. And the next Dread Pirate Roberts has read every word.

Word count: ~760

Ending type used: 1 + 2 — unresolved present / implicating turn (last line 10 words, lands on "word")
